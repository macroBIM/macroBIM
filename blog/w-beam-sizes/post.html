Wide flange beams, called W shapes in American practice and H beams in much of the world, are the I-beams you see in almost every steel building, pipe rack and bridge in the United States. The AISC Shapes Database lists 289 of them, from the 13 lb/ft W4×13 to the 925 lb/ft W36×925, and a size like W12×26 tells you less than you might think: it is not 12 inches deep, and its 26 is a weight, not a dimension.

This guide explains how to read W beam sizes, what every number in the steel tables means and when an engineer actually uses it. It works through a beam selection by hand and compares W shapes with their metric and European IPE and HEA counterparts. It finishes with an interactive table of all 289 shapes in US and SI units. That table includes a lightest-beam finder, a steel weight calculator, side-by-side comparison and a free DXF download for every section.

❝How to read a W beam size

A W designation has two numbers. In W12×26, the first number, 12, is the nominal depth in inches. The second, 26, is the weight in pounds per linear foot. That is all the name tells you. The real dimensions come from the steel tables: W12×26 is actually 12.2 in deep with a 6.49 in wide flange, a 0.38 in flange thickness and a 0.23 in web.

The word "nominal" matters more than most people expect. Steel mills roll each depth group with a fixed set of rolls for the inside of the section and make heavier shapes by opening the rolls, so the flanges and web get thicker and the outside depth grows. For light shapes the actual depth is close to the nominal one. For heavy column shapes it is not: the W14 group runs from 13.7 in (W14×22) to 23.6 in (W14×873).

The practical rule is simple: never detail, clear or coordinate with the nominal depth. Use the tabulated d, and check flange width and thickness as well, because two shapes with the same nominal depth can differ in flange width by a factor of three or more.

▍W, S, M and HP: the four I-shaped families

When people search for "I beam sizes" or "H beam sizes", they usually mean W shapes, but the AISC Manual has four I-shaped families and they are not interchangeable.

  • W (wide flange) has parallel inner flange faces and relatively wide flanges. It is the default choice for beams, girders and columns.
  • S (American Standard) is the original "I-beam": narrow flanges whose inner faces slope at about 16.7 %. S shapes are still used for monorail and crane runway beams, where trolley wheels run on the flange, but they are rarely the economical choice for ordinary framing.
  • M (miscellaneous) covers light, doubly symmetric shapes that cannot be classified as W, S or HP. Availability is limited.
  • HP (bearing pile) shapes have the depth roughly equal to the flange width and the web as thick as the flanges, which makes them robust when driven into the ground as piles.

"H beam" is the common name for a wide flange shape in Asia and the Middle East, and in Europe the closest families are the IPE and HE sections. A W shape with a wide flange, such as W14×90, is what many engineers would call an H section.

❝W beam vs H beam: same steel, different names

If you trained in Korea, Japan, China, the Middle East or Europe, you probably call this section an H beam. In the United States the same rolled section is a W shape. There is no separate "H" family in the AISC tables: every H-shaped American section is listed among the W shapes, next to the deeper, narrower beam-type sizes.

What changes is the proportion. A beam-type W shape such as W24×55 is about three times deeper than it is wide (23.6 in by 7.0 in), because it is meant to resist bending about one axis. A column-type W shape such as W12×65 is square in outline (12.1 in by 12.0 in), because a column needs stiffness about both axes. The second kind is what most of the world calls an H section.

Table 1 — W shape properties and what each one is used for

For a beam bent about its strong axis, three properties do most of the work: Zx for strength, Ix for deflection, and the flange and web proportions for local buckling. For a column, A and the two radii of gyration matter most, and the weak-axis ry is usually the one that governs. J and Cw only appear when a beam is not braced against twisting, but then they decide the answer.

▍Sx or Zx?

Both are section moduli, but they describe different limits. Sx gives the moment at which the extreme fiber first yields, My = FySx. Zx gives the moment when the whole section has yielded, Mp = FyZx. For W shapes the ratio Zx/Sx, called the shape factor, is about 1.1 to 1.2. Under the current AISC Specification, the flexural strength of a compact, laterally braced W shape is based on Zx. Sx is still used for elastic stresses and for lateral-torsional buckling of longer unbraced lengths.

❝Worked example: sizing a simply supported floor beam

The following is a preliminary sizing illustration, not a complete design. A floor beam spans 30 ft between simple supports. The factored uniform load, including an allowance for self-weight, is wu = 3 kip/ft, and the service live load is wL = 1.2 kip/ft. The steel is ASTM A992 (Fy = 50 ksi), and the compression flange is continuously braced by the floor deck.

▍Step 1 — required plastic modulus

Mu = wuL²/8 = 3 × 30² / 8 = 337.5 kip-ft
Zx,req = Mu / (φFy) = 337.5 × 12 / (0.9 × 50) = 90.0 in³

W21×44 is the lightest. Its flanges are compact at 50 ksi (bf/2tf = 7.22 < λp = 0.38√(E/Fy) = 9.15), so φMn = 0.9 × 50 × 95.4 / 12 = 358 kip-ft ≥ 337.5 kip-ft. By contrast, W21×48 in the same list has a non-compact flange at 50 ksi, so its strength would be slightly less than FyZx; it is one of only ten W shapes with that issue.

▍Step 3 — quick deflection check

ΔL = 5wLL⁴ / (384EIx) = 5 × (1.2/12) × (360)⁴ / (384 × 29000 × 843) = 0.89 in
Limit L/360 = 360/360 = 1.00 in  →  0.89 in ≤ 1.00 in  OK

W21×44 passes both checks, with modest margin on deflection. A real design would also check shear, the self-weight assumption, vibration, connections and any unbraced length during construction. The point here is the workflow: strength picks a minimum Zx, deflection picks a minimum Ix, and the lightest shape that satisfies both is normally the economical one. ❝Metric W shapes and European IPE / HEA equivalents

Every W shape also has a soft-metric name, built from the nominal depth in millimetres and the mass in kilograms per metre. W12×26 becomes W310×38.7 and W14×90 becomes W360×134. The depth part comes from the group, not from the actual depth: every W14 is a W360, from 349 mm up to 600 mm deep. The metric mass follows ASTM A6/A6M and is not always the straight conversion of lb/ft. For example, W24×55 is listed as W610×82, not W610×81.8. The tool shows the official metric name for every shape in AISC v15.0. For the six heaviest shapes added in v16.0 it shows a dash, because their metric names are not in that release.

European sections are rolled to different proportions, so there is no exact match. The closest W shapes by mass and stiffness are listed below. IPE beams are slightly lighter and narrower than the matching W shape. HE A sections have very wide flanges, so the closest W shapes come from the W8 and W12 column series.

❝Summary

  • A W designation is nominal depth × weight per foot: W12×26 is about 12 in deep (actually 12.2 in) and weighs 26 lb/ft.
  • Take dimensions from the table, never from the name. Heavy shapes in a group can be much deeper than the nominal depth.
  • Use Zx for the strength of compact braced beams, Ix for deflection, and ry, J and Cw when stability governs.
  • For the example beam (30 ft span, Mu = 337.5 kip-ft) the lightest adequate shape is W21×44.
  • Metric names use nominal mm depth and kg/m mass; IPE and HE A sections have no exact W equivalent.
