Mohr's circle is the picture that turns the stress transformation equations into geometry. Give it the stresses on two perpendicular faces of a small element — σx, σy and τxy — and it tells you, at a glance, the largest and smallest normal stresses anywhere in the material, the planes they act on, and the maximum shear stress.

Most students lose marks on Mohr's circle not because the math is hard, but because of signs: which way to plot the shear, whether the angle on the circle is θ or 2θ, and which way to turn. This post walks through the construction with one clear sign rule, solves a complete example by hand, and ends with an interactive calculator that draws the circle for any stress state you type in.

In this post: what the circle represents, the transformation equations, a sign convention you can trust, a four-step drawing method, a fully worked example in ksi and MPa, an interactive Mohr's circle calculator, the most common mistakes, and where engineers actually use it.

❝What Mohr's circle actually shows

Imagine cutting a tiny square element out of a loaded beam, plate or shaft. On its x-faces there is a normal stress σx and a shear stress τxy; on its y-faces there is σy and the same τxy (shear on perpendicular faces must be equal for the element to stay in equilibrium). Now rotate that element by an angle θ. The stresses on the new faces change — some angles give higher tension, some give more shear.

Every possible orientation of the element corresponds to one point on a single circle drawn in the σ–τ plane. The horizontal coordinate of the point is the normal stress on that face; the vertical coordinate is the shear stress. The two points where the circle crosses the σ axis have zero shear: those are the principal stresses σ1 and σ2. The top and bottom of the circle are the planes of maximum in-plane shear, and the radius of the circle is that maximum shear value.

That is the whole idea. Instead of evaluating trigonometric equations for many angles, you draw one circle and read the extremes off it. (You will also see it called simply the Mohr circle — same thing.)

❝The stress transformation equations

The circle is not a separate theory — it is just a graph of the plane-stress transformation equations. For an element rotated counterclockwise by θ from the x-axis:

σx′ = (σx + σy)/2 + (σx − σy)/2 · cos 2θ + τxy · sin 2θ
τx′y′ = −(σx − σy)/2 · sin 2θ + τxy · cos 2θ

If you square both equations and add them, θ disappears and you are left with the equation of a circle:

(σx′ − C)2 + τx′y′2 = R2
C = (σx + σy)/2     R = √[ ((σx − σy)/2)2 + τxy2 ]

So the center sits on the σ axis at the average normal stress C, and the radius R depends only on the difference between the normal stresses and on the shear. Everything else follows from these two numbers:

σ1, σ2 = C ± R     τmax = R
tan 2θp = 2τxy / (σx − σy)

❝The sign convention (read this first)

The equations above use the standard mechanics sign convention: tension is positive, and τxy is positive when it acts in the +y direction on the positive x-face (and therefore in the +x direction on the positive y-face). The equations are unambiguous. The trouble starts when you plot shear on the circle, because textbooks use different but equivalent rules for the vertical axis.

In this post, and in the calculator below, the rule is:

Clockwise shear is plotted up. Look at the pair of shear stresses on one set of faces. If they form a couple that would turn the element clockwise, plot that face's point above the σ axis; if counterclockwise, plot it below.

A positive τxy pushes up on the right face and down on the left face, which turns the element counterclockwise. So for positive τxy the x-face point X is plotted below the axis at (σx, −τxy), and the y-face point Y is plotted above at (σy, +τxy).

The payoff is simple: with this rule, if the element rotates θ counterclockwise, the point on the circle moves 2θ counterclockwise. Same direction, double the angle. You never have to remember a "reverse the direction" exception.

❝How to draw Mohr's circle in four steps

  1. Draw the axes and mark the center. Horizontal axis σ (tension to the right), vertical axis τ (clockwise up). The center is at C = (σx + σy)/2 on the σ axis.
  2. Plot the two face points. X = (σx, shear on the x-face) and Y = (σy, shear on the y-face), using the clockwise-up rule. X and Y always lie on opposite ends of a diameter, so the line X–Y passes through C — a quick check that you plotted them correctly.
  3. Draw the circle. The radius is the distance from C to X: R = √[((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy²].
  4. Read the answers. The right and left intersections with the σ axis are σ1 = C + R and σ2 = C − R. The angle on the circle from X to σ1 is 2θp; the element turns half of that, in the same direction. The top and bottom points give τmax = R, and on those planes the normal stress is C, not zero.

❝Worked example: principal stresses step by step

A point in a steel plate carries σx = 12 ksi (tension), σy = −4 ksi (compression) and τxy = +6 ksi. Find the principal stresses and their directions, the maximum in-plane shear, and the stresses on a face rotated 30° counterclockwise.

▍Center and radius

C = (12 + (−4)) / 2 = 4 ksi
R = √[ ((12 − (−4))/2)2 + 62 ] = √(82 + 62) = 10 ksi
σ1 = 4 + 10 = 14 ksi     σ2 = 4 − 10 = −6 ksi

The positive shear on the x-face turns the element counterclockwise, so X is plotted below the axis at (12, −6) and Y above it at (−4, +6). From the center, X sits 8 units to the right and 6 units down, so the angle between C–X and the σ axis is atan(6/8) = 36.87°.

❝Principal Stresses and Maximum Shear Stress from the Circle

Once the circle is drawn, the principal stresses and the maximum shear stress are simply its extreme points: the far right and far left of the circle on the σ axis, and its top and bottom. For our example that gives σ1 = 14 ksi, σ2 = −6 ksi and τmax = 10 ksi. What the Mohr circle adds beyond those three numbers is the direction of each one.

▍Principal direction

To go from X to σ1 on the circle we turn 2θp = 36.87° counterclockwise. The element therefore turns θp = 18.43° counterclockwise: on faces whose normal is 18.4° above the x-axis, the stress is pure tension of 14 ksi, and on the perpendicular faces it is pure compression of 6 ksi. The formula agrees: tan 2θp = 2(6) / (12 − (−4)) = 0.75, so 2θp = 36.87°.

▍Maximum in-plane shear

The top of the circle is 90° on the circle — 45° on the element — away from the principal points, so the planes of maximum shear are at θs = θp − 45° = −26.6° (that is, 26.6° clockwise) and at 63.4° counterclockwise. The maximum in-plane shear is τmax = R = 10 ksi, and the normal stress on those planes is the center value, 4 ksi. Forgetting that normal stress is a classic mistake when sketching the max-shear element.

❝Stress on a Rotated Plane

This is the stress transformation done graphically. Rotating the element 30° counterclockwise moves the point 60° counterclockwise around the circle, from X at −36.87° to X′ at +23.13°. Reading coordinates off the circle:

σx′ = 4 + 10 cos 23.13° = 13.20 ksi
plotted shear = 10 sin 23.13° = 3.93 (above the axis → clockwise) ⇒ τx′y′ = −3.93 ksi
σy′ = 4 − 10 cos 23.13° = −5.20 ksi

The transformation equations give the same result directly: σx′ = 4 + 8 cos 60° + 6 sin 60° = 13.20 ksi and τx′y′ = −8 sin 60° + 6 cos 60° = −3.93 ksi. A quick sanity check that never fails: σx′ + σy′ = 13.20 − 5.20 = 8 = σx + σy. The sum of normal stresses is the same on every orientation.

▍In-plane versus absolute maximum shear

The radius R is the largest shear stress in the plane of the element. Real material is three-dimensional, and for plane stress the third principal stress is zero. If σ1 and σ2 have opposite signs, as in our example, R is also the absolute maximum. But if both are tensile (say 30 and 10 ksi), the largest shear acts on a plane tilted out of the x–y plane and equals σ1/2 = 15 ksi, larger than the in-plane R = 10 ksi. Drawing the two extra circles through the origin makes this obvious, and it matters for yield checks such as Tresca.

❝Where engineers actually use it

Outside the classroom, few designers draw Mohr's circles by hand, but the idea behind them is everywhere:

  • Girder webs. Near the supports of a plate girder or a prestressed concrete beam, the web carries bending stress and shear at the same time. Principal tension in the web is what drives diagonal cracking in concrete, and principal stresses feed the von Mises check in steel.
  • Bolts, welds and connections. A fillet weld or a bolt group under combined shear and tension is checked with an interaction that comes from the same transformation.
  • Strain gauge rosettes. Field measurements on bridges give three strains in different directions; converting them into principal strains and stresses is the strain version of Mohr's circle.
  • Soil and rock. Geotechnical engineers use Mohr's circle together with the Mohr–Coulomb failure envelope to judge when soil will slip.

Software does these calculations in the background, but when a principal stress result looks strange, a quick circle on paper is still the fastest way to check whether the input signs make sense.

❝Summary

  • Mohr's circle is the graph of the plane-stress transformation equations: center C = (σx + σy)/2, radius R = √[((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy²].
  • Principal stresses are C ± R; maximum in-plane shear is R, acting with normal stress C.
  • Plot clockwise shear up, and the element rotates θ in the same direction as the 2θ rotation on the circle.
  • For the example (12, −4, 6 ksi): σ1 = 14 ksi, σ2 = −6 ksi, θp = 18.4° CCW, τmax = 10 ksi.
