If you have ever load-rated an older highway bridge in the United States, there is a good chance its superstructure is a row of precast, prestressed concrete I-beams of one of six standard AASHTO shapes. They are often simply called AASHTO girders or AASHTO Type I to Type VI beams.

The shapes are easy to find as scanned standard sheets, but much harder to find in a form you can actually use: one table with every dimension, section properties you can check, and a CAD outline you can drop into a drawing. This post puts all three in one place.

In this post: the dimension table for all six types (inches and SI), section properties I recalculated and checked against the PCI Bridge Design Manual, an interactive section-property calculator, a comparison with AASHTO-PCI bulb-tees, and free DXF downloads of every shape.

❝What is an AASHTO I-beam girder?

An AASHTO I-beam is a pretensioned concrete girder cast in a precasting plant, shipped to site, and set side by side under a cast-in-place deck. The deck is placed on top and acts compositely with the girders. The six shapes were standardized jointly by AASHTO and the Precast/Prestressed Concrete Institute (PCI) so that fabricators could reuse the same steel forms on many projects.

The family grows from a 28-inch Type I for short spans to a 72-inch Type VI for long ones. Types I through IV share the same look: a narrow top flange, a single 45-degree-ish taper into the web, and a wide, heavy bottom flange that holds the prestressing strands. Types V and VI were added for longer spans and have a much wider (42-inch) top flange with a two-stage taper.

▍Where you will still meet them

  • Existing bridges. A large share of the prestressed girder bridges built in the U.S. in the second half of the 20th century use these shapes, so they come up constantly in inspection, rating and widening work.
  • Widenings and replacements. When an old span is widened, matching the existing girder type keeps the stiffness and deck profile consistent.
  • Teaching and exams. They remain the default example for prestressed concrete design in many textbooks.

For new bridges, many state DOTs now prefer more efficient shapes such as the AASHTO-PCI bulb-tee or their own families (NU girders, Florida I-beams, Texas Tx girders). We will see why in the comparison below.

▍Reading the table quickly

  • Depth (D1): 28, 36, 45, 54, 63 and 72 in. — roughly 0.7 m to 1.8 m.
  • Web (B3): only 6 to 8 in. thick, which is why web shear and strand debonding details deserve attention in rating work.
  • Bottom flange (B2 × D6): grows from 16 × 5 in. to 28 × 8 in. — this is where the strands live.
  • Clear web height (D1 minus all flange parts) is 11, 15, 19, 23, 33 and 42 in. for Types I–VI.

Actual forms also have small corner chamfers (typically around 3/4 in.) that are not in the table; they change the properties by a negligible amount.

Table 4 — Same depth, different efficiency. I / A is a quick measure of stiffness per unit of concrete.

At the same 54-inch depth, a BT-54 is slightly stiffer than a Type IV while using about 16% less concrete. At 72 inches, the BT-72 carries about 74% of the Type VI inertia with only 71% of its weight. The thin web and wide top flange of the bulb-tee put the material where it works hardest, and the lighter weight also means longer pieces can be shipped and erected with the same cranes. That, more than anything, is why newer projects tend to move away from Types I–IV.

The AASHTO shapes still have advantages: their heavy bottom flange holds many strands for its depth, the thick flanges are forgiving in handling, and for widening an existing bridge there is often no real choice.
