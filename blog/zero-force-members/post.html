A zero-force member is a truss member that carries no axial force under a particular set of loads. Spotting them before you start the method of joints or the method of sections removes unknowns, shortens any truss analysis done by hand, and is one of the first skills tested in statics and structural analysis exams.

The good news is that you rarely need to calculate anything to find them. Two simple rules, both coming straight from equilibrium at a single joint, catch almost every zero-force member in a typical bridge or roof truss. This post explains the rules and why they work, then walks through three worked examples, including one case the rules cannot see. It ends with an interactive quiz where you click the zero-force members yourself and get a step-by-step solution.

In this post: the two rules and the loaded-joint exception, a step-by-step procedure, three worked examples with full member forces, why zero-force members are still needed in real trusses, the most common mistakes, and a quiz with random trusses.

❝What is a zero-force member?

The usual truss model makes three assumptions: members are straight, joints are frictionless pins, and loads act only at the joints. Under those assumptions every member is a two-force member, so it carries only axial tension or compression. Each joint is then a small particle in equilibrium, which means two equations must hold at every joint: the forces add up to zero horizontally (ΣFx = 0) and vertically (ΣFy = 0).

A zero-force member appears when the geometry of a joint makes it impossible for one member to be balanced by anything else. If a member is the only thing at a joint that can push or pull in a certain direction, and there is no load or reaction in that direction, its force has to be zero. The rules below are just this observation written down for the two joint shapes that come up most often.

The examples below use the three truss layouts you will meet most often: the Pratt truss (verticals in compression, diagonals sloping down toward the middle), the Howe truss (the mirror image, with diagonals sloping up toward the middle), and the Warren truss (alternating diagonals, here with extra verticals). The rules work the same way for all of them.

Keep in mind that “zero” always refers to a specific load case. The same member can be zero under one set of loads and heavily loaded under another, which is exactly why engineers never delete them from a real structure.

❝The rules for finding zero-force members

Joint conditionResult
Rule 1No load, no support, exactly two members that are not collinearBoth members are zero
Rule 2No load, no support, exactly three members, two of them collinearThe third (non-collinear) member is zero
ExceptionSame as Rule 2, but a load P acts along the third memberThe third member carries P, not zero
Not allowedAny joint with a support reactionDo not use the rules — the reaction can balance the member

Table 1 — Zero-force member rules at a joint

▍Why Rule 1 works

Take a joint with only two members, A and B, that do not lie on the same line, and nothing else acting on it. Write the equilibrium equation in the direction perpendicular to member A. Member A has no component in that direction, so the only term left is the component of member B, which is FB sin θ, where θ is the angle between the members. Since sin θ is not zero, FB must be zero. Repeat the argument perpendicular to B and FA is zero as well.

▍Why Rule 2 works

Now take a joint with three members, where two of them lie on one straight line (for example two segments of a continuous chord) and the third comes in at an angle. Sum forces perpendicular to the straight line. The two collinear members have no component in that direction; only the third member does. With no load at the joint, that single component must vanish, so the third member is a zero-force member. Summing along the line then shows that the two collinear members carry the same force.

▍The loaded joint exception

If a load acts at the joint, the argument changes. Suppose a load P acts along the direction of the third member, such as a floor-beam reaction hanging from a vertical. Summing perpendicular to the chord now gives F3 = P instead of zero. This is why the rules are stated only for unloaded joints, and why the position of the loads decides which members are zero.

▍The chain effect

Once you have found a zero-force member, you can treat it as if it were not there for the current load case. A neighbouring joint that used to have four members may now have three with two collinear, so Rule 2 suddenly applies. Always go around the truss a second time after the first set of zeros.

❝How to find zero-force members with the method of joints

The method of joints is the standard hand method of truss analysis: you isolate one joint at a time, draw its free-body diagram, and solve ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 for at most two unknown member forces. Finding each zero-force member first is simply the method of joints applied to the easiest joints, without writing the numbers down.

For example, at joint U2 of the Pratt truss in Example 1(a), take the members U1–U2 and U2–U3 along the x-axis and the vertical L2–U2 along the y-axis. With no load at U2, ΣFy = 0 gives FL2U2 = 0 immediately, and ΣFx = 0 gives FU1U2 = FU2U3. That is Rule 2 in two lines of algebra. A practical order of work:

  1. Mark every joint that has a support or an external load. The rules are not used at these joints.
  2. At each remaining joint, count the members. Two members not on one line → both zero (Rule 1).
  3. Three members with two on one line → the third is zero (Rule 2).
  4. Remove the members you found and repeat steps 2–3 at the neighbouring joints until nothing changes.
  5. If the truss and its loads are symmetric, check the middle panels with the method of sections; zero shear means zero diagonal force (Example 3).
  6. Continue the method of joints for the remaining members, starting at a support joint where only two forces are unknown.

❝Example 1: the same Pratt truss with two load positions

Consider a four-panel Pratt truss with 12 ft panels and a depth of 9 ft. In case (a) a 20-kip load acts at each of the three bottom-chord joints, as it would when the deck hangs from the bottom chord. In case (b) the same loads act at the top-chord joints instead, like a deck-type bridge or a roof.

In case (a), joints U1, U2 and U3 are unloaded. Joint U2 has the two top-chord members on one line and the vertical L2–U2 as the third member, so Rule 2 makes L2–U2 a zero-force member — the joint calculation shown above. Joints U1 and U3 each have four members (end diagonal, top chord, vertical and inner diagonal), so neither rule applies there.

In case (b), the bottom-chord joints are unloaded. At L1 and L3 the bottom chord is straight and the vertical is the third member, so L1–U1 and L3–U3 are zero. Joint L2 has five members, so it is not covered by either rule, and L2–U2 now carries the full 20-kip load from U2 in compression.

The table also confirms something useful: the chords and diagonals do not change at all between the two cases. Moving the loads from one chord to the other only changes the verticals, and each vertical is either a hanger carrying 20 kips or a zero-force member.

❝Example 2: Howe truss and Warren truss with verticals

In the Howe truss with 20 kips at each top-chord joint, the inner diagonals run from L1 and L3 up to the middle top joint U2, so no diagonal reaches joint L2. That joint has only the two bottom-chord members and the vertical. Rule 2 gives L2–U2 = 0. Joints L1 and L3 each have a diagonal arriving as well, so they have four members and stay loaded.

The Warren truss with verticals is a common pattern for pedestrian bridges and roof trusses. With loads only at the main bottom joints L2, L4 and L6, the joints L1, L3, L5 and L7 are unloaded, have a straight bottom chord, and a single vertical. All four verticals are zero-force members. Designers add these verticals to shorten the top chord's buckling length or to pick up floor beams placed at those joints, which brings us to why they are not useless.

❝Example 3: zero-force members the rules cannot see

Load the same Warren truss with 20 kips at each of the four top-chord joints. Rule 2 again finds the four verticals at L1, L3, L5 and L7. But the full solution shows two more zero-force members: the middle diagonals U1–L4 and L4–U2. Joint L4 has four members, so no joint rule applies.

The reason is shear. The loads are symmetric, so each support takes 40 kips and the shear in the middle panels is 40 − 20 − 20 = 0. In a parallel-chord truss the diagonal in a panel carries the panel shear divided by sin θ, so zero shear means a zero diagonal. Exam questions that ask “how many zero-force members are there?” sometimes count these, so when the answer choices look off, cut a section through the middle panel and check.

In bridge practice this is the normal situation. Live loads move, so a member that is zero under dead load is checked under every truck position. Light verticals in a Warren truss often exist mainly to brace the top chord, and their size is governed by stiffness and minimum section rules rather than by the analysis force.

❝Common mistakes

MistakeWhat to check
Applying the rules at a supportJoints L0 and the roller end have reactions; a lone member there is usually loaded.
Ignoring a load at the jointA loaded joint breaks both rules. Check the direction of the load first.
Stopping after one passAfter deleting a zero-force member, revisit the neighbouring joints — new zeros often appear.
Calling “almost collinear” collinearRule 2 needs exactly the same line. A slight kink in the chord means the third member is not automatically zero.
Removing zero-force members from the designThey are zero for one load case only and often brace compression members. Keep them.

❝Summary

  • At an unloaded, unsupported joint with two non-collinear members, both are zero-force members.
  • At an unloaded, unsupported joint with three members, two of them collinear, the third is a zero-force member.
  • A load along the third member makes it carry that load instead; supports also break the rules.
  • Remove each zero-force member and check the neighbouring joints again; use symmetry and zero shear for the rest.
  • Zero-force members depend on the load case and still brace, stabilise and carry other loads, so they stay in the design.
