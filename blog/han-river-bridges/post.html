Seoul is split in two by the Han River (Hangang), and the city is stitched back together by bridges. According to the Seoul Metropolitan Government, there are 32 bridges across the main stream of the Han River today, from the first railway bridge of 1900 to a record-breaking concrete cable-stayed bridge that opened in January 2025.

This guide lists all 32 bridges in order from upstream to downstream, with the year each opened, its length, its longest span and its structural type. I am a bridge engineer, so instead of night-view photos you will find the engineering side: why one bridge is a truss and another a cable-stayed bridge, and how 120 years of bridge technology are lined up along one river.

Quick facts
• 32 bridges on the main stream (28 inside Seoul, 4 in Gyeonggi Province), Paldang Bridge → Ilsan Bridge
• Most are girder bridges (steel box, steel plate, prestressed concrete box); 4 cable-stayed, 6 arch-type, 5 truss-type, and no suspension bridge
• Oldest: Hangang Railway Bridge (1900) · Newest: Godeok-Topyeong Bridge (2025, 540 m main span)

❝How many bridges cross the Han River?

The Seoul Metropolitan Government's bridge statistics (as of 31 December 2025) count 32 bridges. Only the main stream below Dumulmeori, where the North and South Han rivers meet, is counted. The easternmost is Paldang Bridge and the westernmost is Ilsan Bridge.

❝Map of all 32 Han River bridges

The bridges are packed most tightly in central Seoul, between Jamsil in the east and Yeouido island in the west. In this stretch the river is about 900–1,200 m wide, and a bridge appears roughly every kilometre.

Some bridges combine types (Banghwa is an arch truss over a steel box approach), so they are counted under their main type. Interestingly, there is no suspension bridge on the Han River.

❝Longest spans, drawn to the same scale

Godeok-Topyeong Bridge stands out with a 540 m main span. According to its builder, Hyundai E&C, it is the longest-span concrete cable-stayed bridge in the world. The first Han River bridge, in 1900, crossed with a chain of 61 m trusses, so the distance covered in a single span has grown about nine times in 125 years.

A bridge type is chosen by span, loads, construction conditions, and the technology and budget of its time, not by looks alone. That is why bridges built in the same decade tend to look alike.

❝Stories behind the bridges

  • Hangang Railway Bridge (1900) – the first bridge over the Han River, built for the Seoul–Incheon railway. Today four parallel railway bridges stand side by side. It was blown up on 28 June 1950, during the Korean War, and later rebuilt.
  • Hangang Bridge (1917) – the first road bridge, originally a footbridge. Widened with tied arches in 1936, blown up in 1950 and restored in 1958.
  • Gwangjin Bridge (1936) – the second road bridge. Demolished in 1994 because of age, rebuilt in 2003; part of the deck is now a walking and cycling path.
  • Seongsu Bridge (1979 → 1997) – on 21 October 1994 a 48 m suspended truss section fell into the river, killing 32 people. Fatigue cracks at poorly welded hanger connections were the direct cause. The disaster led to Korea's Special Act on the Safety Control of Public Structures (1995).
  • Paldang Bridge – planned as a concrete cable-stayed bridge, it collapsed in strong winds during construction in 1991. It was redesigned as a girder bridge and opened in 1995.
  • New Haengju Bridge – collapsed during construction in 1992, rebuilt and opened in 1995.
  • Olympic Bridge (1989) – built for the 1988 Seoul Olympics; its four towers are 88 m tall.
  • Godeok-Topyeong Bridge (2025) – 540 m main span, the world's longest concrete cable-stayed span according to its builder.
