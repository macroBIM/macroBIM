Most deflection methods ask you to integrate a differential equation or memorize a table of beam formulas. Castigliano's theorem — often called Castigliano's method — takes a different route: write down how much elastic energy the structure stores, differentiate it with respect to a load, and the result is the deflection under that load. The same three-line procedure works for cantilevers, simply supported beams, frames, trusses and even statically indeterminate structures.

This post explains the theorem from the energy idea upward, shows the dummy-load trick that lets you find deflection anywhere, and works four complete examples in U.S. units. At the end there is a beam deflection calculator that shows every step of the integral, so you can check your homework or a quick hand calculation.

In this post: the strain energy expressions you actually need, Castigliano's second theorem and the dummy (fictitious) load method, worked examples for a cantilever, a simply supported beam, a three-bar truss and a propped cantilever, common mistakes, and a step-by-step beam deflection calculator.

❝What Castigliano's theorem says

When a load is applied slowly to a linear elastic structure, the work done by the load is stored as strain energy U. For a single load P that moves through a deflection Δ, the load–deflection line is straight, so the stored energy is the triangle under it: U = ½PΔ. Because Δ = P/k, the energy can be written purely in terms of the load, U = P²/(2k), and differentiating gives ∂U/∂P = P/k = Δ.

Carlo Alberto Castigliano generalized this observation to structures carrying many loads and published it in 1879. His second theorem is the one engineers use for deflections:

Δi = ∂U / ∂Pideflection at the point and in the direction of load Pi
θi = ∂U / ∂Mirotation at the point of applied moment Mi

Here U must be written as a function of the loads, not of the displacements. The theorem holds for linear elastic material and small deflections, which covers almost all serviceability checks in steel and concrete design. It does not include temperature or support settlement effects directly; those are easier to handle with the unit load (virtual work) method.

❝Strain energy in structural members

Every internal force stores energy. For a member of length L, the four contributions are:

ActionStrain energy UTypical importance
Axial force NN²L / (2AE), or ∫ N²/(2AE) dxGoverns trusses and cables
Bending moment M∫ M²/(2EI) dxGoverns beams and frames
Shear force V∫ fsV²/(2GA) dxSmall for slender beams (span/depth > 10)
Torque T∫ T²/(2GJ) dxCurved girders, grids, shafts

Table 1 — Strain energy terms. fs is the shear form factor (1.2 for a rectangle).

In ordinary beam problems only the bending term is kept; in pin-jointed trusses only the axial term exists. Mixing them up is one of the most common errors, so decide at the start which terms your structure really needs.

▍Differentiate under the integral sign

Squaring M(x), integrating and then differentiating is slow. Because the limits of integration do not depend on the load, you can differentiate first. This gives the working forms used in every example below:

Δ = ∂U/∂P = (1/EI) ∫ M(x) · (∂M/∂P) dxbeams and frames
Δ = Σ N · (∂N/∂P) · L / (AE)trusses

The term ∂M/∂P is simply the bending moment produced by a unit value of P. That is exactly the "virtual moment" m(x) of the unit load method, which is why the two methods always give identical answers: Castigliano's theorem is the energy route, the unit load method is the virtual work route, and the arithmetic is the same.

❝The dummy load trick

The theorem gives the deflection at a point where a load acts. To find deflection where there is no load — midspan of a beam under uniform load, or the horizontal movement of a roller — add an imaginary load Q at that point, in the direction you want. Write M(x) including Q, take ∂M/∂Q, and only then set Q = 0. The derivative keeps the effect of Q on the energy even though Q itself vanishes.

Order matters: differentiate first, then set Q = 0. If you put Q = 0 before differentiating, ∂M/∂Q disappears and the deflection comes out as zero.

❝Example 1 — Cantilever beam deflection with end load and uniform load

Measure x from the free end so that no reactions are needed. With w = 0.5 kip/ft = 0.04167 kip/in and L = 120 in, the moment and its derivative are:

M(x) = −P x − w x²/2
∂M/∂P = −x
Δtip = (1/EI) ∫0L (P x² + w x³/2) dx = PL³/(3EI) + wL⁴/(8EI)

The uniform load has no force at the tip, yet its effect is captured: it appears in M(x), and ∂M/∂P picks it up during integration.

❝Example 2 — Simply supported beam, midspan, dummy load

With Q at midspan each reaction is wL/2 + Q/2. By symmetry, integrate over the left half and double it:

M(x) = (wL/2 + Q/2) x − w x²/2, 0 ≤ x ≤ L/2
∂M/∂Q = x/2
Δmid = (2/EI) ∫0L/2 (wLx/2 − wx²/2)(x/2) dx (Q = 0) = 5wL⁴/(384EI)

With w = 0.1 kip/in and L = 288 in: Δmid = 5(0.1)(288)⁴ / (384 · 29,000 · 800) = 0.386 in, or L/746 — comfortably inside a typical L/360 live-load limit.

❝Example 3 — Truss joint deflection

For a truss the energy is ΣN²L/(2AE), so the deflection is ΣN(∂N/∂P)L/(AE). Solve the member forces once with the method of joints, then divide each by P to get ∂N/∂P. For the horizontal movement of roller C, add a dummy horizontal load Q at C; only member AC feels it, with ∂NAC/∂Q = 1.

❝Example 4 — Propped cantilever (statically indeterminate)

A support that does not move does no work, so the derivative of the energy with respect to its reaction must be zero. Remove the roller, keep its reaction R as an applied load, and impose ∂U/∂R = 0. Measuring x from the roller end:

M(x) = R x − w x²/2, ∂M/∂R = x
∂U/∂R = (1/EI) ∫0L (R x² − w x³/2) dx = (1/EI)(RL³/3 − wL⁴/8) = 0
R = 3wL/8 = 3(2)(20)/8 = 15 kip

The fixed end then carries 2(20) − 15 = 25 kip and a moment of wL²/8 = 100 kip·ft. The same idea extends to two or more redundants: one equation ∂U/∂Rj = 0 per unknown.

❝Common mistakes

  • Setting the dummy load to zero too early. Differentiate with respect to Q first; only then substitute Q = 0.
  • Inconsistent sign conventions. M(x) and ∂M/∂P must use the same convention over the whole beam. A sign slip in one segment can cancel half the answer.
  • Forgetting segments. A point load or a dummy load splits the beam; write M(x) separately on each side and integrate each part.
  • Mixing units. Convert w to kip/in when L is in inches. Leaving w in kip/ft while L is in inches makes the uniform-load part 12 times too large.
  • Using the wrong energy terms. Bending for beams, axial for trusses. Adding axial energy to a beam is rarely wrong, but forgetting bending in a frame is.
  • Applying it to nonlinear problems. Cracked concrete, yielding steel and large deflections break the linear energy relation the theorem relies on.

❝Where engineers still use it

Nobody designs a bridge girder with hand integrals any more, yet Castigliano's method earns its place in daily practice. It is the fastest independent check of finite element results: one dummy load and one integral tell you whether the program's midspan deflection is in the right range. Bridge engineers use the same energy expressions to estimate camber of steel and precast girders under dead load, to check truss bridge deflections member by member, and to find redundant forces in small indeterminate details such as a propped stringer or a tied arch hanger. For students, it is often the method that makes indeterminate structures click, because one rule — the support does no work — replaces a page of compatibility equations.

Castigliano's method and the virtual work method (unit load method) always give the same numbers; the choice is about convenience. Virtual work handles temperature change and support settlement more directly, while Castigliano's theorem is quicker when the loads are already symbolic or when you need a redundant reaction.

❝Summary

  • Deflection equals the derivative of strain energy with respect to the load at that point: Δ = ∂U/∂P.
  • Differentiate under the integral: Δ = (1/EI)∫M(∂M/∂P)dx for beams, Σ N(∂N/∂P)L/AE for trusses.
  • Use a dummy load Q where no real load acts, and set Q = 0 only after differentiating.
  • For a redundant reaction, ∂U/∂R = 0 gives the extra equation — R = 3wL/8 for a uniformly loaded propped cantilever.
