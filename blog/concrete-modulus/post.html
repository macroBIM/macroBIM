The modulus of elasticity of concrete, Ec — also called Young's modulus of concrete — is one of those numbers that quietly drives a lot of design work. It sets how much a slab deflects, how much a prestressed girder cambers at release, how much prestress is lost to elastic shortening, and how much area the steel "counts for" in a cracked section. Yet most engineers never measure it — they take it from a code formula based on compressive strength.

This post collects the formulas you are most likely to need — ACI 318, Eurocode 2 and AASHTO LRFD — explains what the code value actually represents, compares the three side by side, and works through examples in US and SI units. At the end there is a concrete modulus of elasticity calculator that gives Ec and the modular ratio for any strength.

❝What the modulus of elasticity of concrete really is

Concrete is not a linear material. Its stress–strain curve bends over almost from the start, because microcracks at the paste–aggregate interface begin to grow under quite low stress. So "the" modulus of elasticity depends on where on the curve you measure it. Three definitions are common:

  • Initial tangent modulus — the slope at the origin. It is the stiffest value and is mainly used in research and dynamic problems.
  • Tangent modulus — the slope at a given stress. It falls steadily as the stress rises toward f′c.
  • Secant modulus — the slope of a straight line from the origin to a point on the curve. This is what design codes mean by Ec.

ACI 318 describes Ec as the slope of the line from zero stress to a compressive stress of 0.45 f′c; ASTM C469, the standard US test, measures a chord modulus in the same low-stress range. Eurocode 2 defines Ecm as the secant between σc = 0 and 0.4 fcm. In both cases the idea is the same: a single straight line that represents concrete behavior at service stresses, which is where stiffness matters most.

❝ACI 318 formula for concrete modulus of elasticity

ACI 318-19 gives the modulus of elasticity in Section 19.2.2.1. The general form includes the unit weight of the concrete, so it covers lightweight, sand-lightweight and normalweight mixes:

Ec = wc1.5 · 33 · √f′c   (psi)   for wc = 90 to 160 lb/ft³
Ec = wc1.5 · 0.043 · √f′c   (MPa)   for wc = 1,440 to 2,560 kg/m³

For normalweight concrete the code permits a simpler expression that does not need the unit weight:

Ec = 57,000 · √f′c   (psi)
Ec = 4,700 · √f′c   (MPa)

The simplified version corresponds to a unit weight of roughly 145 lb/ft³. If your concrete is heavier — typical bridge and precast mixes with reinforcement are often closer to 150 lb/ft³ — the general form gives a slightly higher value. Both are empirical fits to test data, not theory, which is why the square root of strength appears: stiffness grows with strength, but much more slowly.

▍Example 1 — ACI, US units

A normalweight concrete with f′c = 5,000 psi and a measured unit weight of 150 lb/ft³:

General: Ec = 1501.5 × 33 × √5,000 = 1,837 × 33 × 70.7 = 4,287 ksi
Simplified: Ec = 57,000 × √5,000 = 4,031 ksi
Modular ratio with Es = 29,000 ksi: n = 29,000 / 4,287 = 6.8

The two answers differ by about 6%, simply because the simplified form assumes a lighter concrete. For most building work this difference is lost in the scatter discussed below; for precast girder camber it is worth using the general form with the actual unit weight.

❝Eurocode 2 formula for Ecm

EN 1992-1-1 (Table 3.1) relates the mean secant modulus to the mean compressive strength rather than the characteristic one:

Ecm = 22 · (fcm / 10)0.3   (GPa),    fcm = fck + 8 MPa

The formula is calibrated for quartzite aggregates. Clause 3.1.3(2) tells you to reduce the value by 10% for limestone and by 30% for sandstone, and to increase it by 20% for basalt. That is a much larger effect than the difference between design codes, and it is a useful reminder that the aggregate, not the cement paste, controls most of the stiffness.

▍Example 2 — Eurocode 2, SI units

C30/37: fcm = 30 + 8 = 38 MPa
Ecm = 22 × (3.8)0.3 = 32.8 GPa  (quartzite)
Limestone aggregate: 0.9 × 32.8 = 29.6 GPa

Note that the Eurocode value for C30/37 (32.8 GPa) is noticeably higher than ACI's 4,700√30 = 25.7 GPa for the same cylinder strength. Part of the gap is the use of fcm instead of fck, and part is the quartzite reference. With limestone aggregate the two codes come much closer.

❝AASHTO LRFD formula — for bridge engineers

AASHTO LRFD Article 5.4.2.4 was revised (7th Edition with 2015 Interims) to an equation based on a much larger database that includes high-strength concrete:

Ec = 120,000 · K1 · wc2.0 · f′c0.33   (ksi)   wc in kcf, f′c in ksi

K1 is a correction factor for the source of aggregate, taken as 1.0 unless physical tests show otherwise. Because the exponent on strength is 0.33 instead of 0.5, this equation grows more slowly at high strength than ACI's square root — which matches test data on high-strength girder concrete better. Some state DOTs use their own expressions for high-strength pretensioned beams, so check the bridge manual you are working under.

▍Example 3 — prestressed girder, elastic shortening

A pretensioned girder has f′c = 8.0 ksi at 28 days and f′ci = 6.0 ksi at release. Take wc = 0.150 kcf and K1 = 1.0:

Ec = 120,000 × 1.0 × 0.1502 × 8.00.33 = 5,363 ksi
Eci = 120,000 × 1.0 × 0.1502 × 6.00.33 = 4,877 ksi
Elastic shortening loss: ΔfpES = (Ep / Eci) · fcgp = (28,500 / 4,877) × 2.0 ksi = 11.7 ksi

Here fcgp = 2.0 ksi is an assumed concrete stress at the strand centroid. If the real Eci turns out 15% lower than the formula — not unusual with softer aggregates — the loss rises to 13.7 ksi, and camber at release increases by roughly the same proportion. That is why many precasters measure Eci for their own mixes.

Three things stand out. First, below about 6,000 psi the ACI and AASHTO lines are close; above that, AASHTO's flatter curve gives lower values, while the Eurocode quartzite line stays highest throughout. Second, the spread between codes (roughly 10–20%) is smaller than the spread from aggregate type alone. Third, none of these numbers should be quoted to four significant figures — they are estimates of a property that varies from batch to batch.

❝Why measured values scatter

Measured values commonly land within roughly ±20% of the code formula, and the formulas themselves were fitted to scattered test data. In practice the main reasons are:

  • Aggregate type and volume — stiff aggregates such as basalt or dense limestone raise Ec; sandstone and lightweight aggregates lower it. More coarse aggregate per cubic yard also means a stiffer concrete.
  • Moisture — wet specimens test stiffer than dry ones, because water in the pores carries part of the load.
  • Age and curing — Ec develops faster than strength at early ages; at release of a precast girder it may be closer to the 28-day value than the strength ratio suggests.
  • Test method — loading rate, specimen size and how the chord is defined all shift the result by a few percent.
Rule of thumb: if a deflection, camber or prestress-loss check is close to its limit, run it with Ec ±20% before relying on it — or ask for ASTM C469 tests on the actual mix.

❝Where Ec matters in design

  • Deflection — immediate deflection is inversely proportional to Ec·I, so a 15% error in Ec is a 15% error in deflection.
  • Modular ratio — n = Es/Ec converts steel into equivalent concrete area for cracked-section and transformed-section analysis.
  • Prestress losses and camber — elastic shortening and camber at release both depend on Eci.
  • Frame analysis — relative stiffness of members, drift and P-delta effects all use Ec.

❝Summary

  • Code values of Ec are secant moduli at service stress (about 0.4–0.45 f′c), not the initial slope.
  • ACI 318: Ec = wc1.5·33√f′c psi, or 57,000√f′c psi for normalweight concrete.
  • Eurocode 2: Ecm = 22(fcm/10)0.3 GPa, adjusted −10% for limestone, −30% for sandstone, +20% for basalt.
  • AASHTO LRFD: Ec = 120,000·K1·wc2.0·f′c0.33 ksi — flatter at high strength.
  • Expect real values within about ±20% of any formula; test the mix when deflection, camber or losses govern.
